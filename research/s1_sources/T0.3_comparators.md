**Route that worked:** WebSearch, multiple queries per country.

**Verbatim extract / findings by country:**

**Pakistan** — the Export Bonus Voucher Scheme is confirmed as a real, dated, partial liberalisation:
> "The Export Bonus Scheme was introduced by the Government of Pakistan via Ministry of Commerce Public Notice dated the 15th January, 1959." ... "The regime introduced the Export Bonus Voucher Scheme in January 1959, granting exporters redeemable vouchers worth 20–40% of foreign exchange earnings for importing capital goods and raw materials."

But it was only partial and the broader picture is a liberalise-then-reverse pattern:
> "The 1960s was a period of import liberalization, market decontrol, and export encouragement" under Ayub Khan, with average GDP growth of 6.8%. But: "The early 1970s were marked by devaluation and tariff rationalization and were followed by import restrictions and export subsidies in the late 1970s... peak in inward orientation under Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's nationalization drive, which expanded state control over key sectors and reinforced barriers to imports through escalated tariffs and bans." The Export Bonus Scheme itself "was abandoned in 1972 after the devaluation of Pakistani rupee."

**Ceylon** — confirms the brief's own prediction: no 1953 sunset found, and substantive liberalisation dated to 1977:
> "In 1977, Sri Lanka responded to the dismal economic outcome of the closed-economy era by embarking on an extensive economic liberalization process, becoming the first country in the South Asian region to do so." ... "The exchange system of Sri Lanka underwent a fundamental change during 1977. The new UNP government began to unwind the entire paraphernalia of administered controls on consumption, investment and foreign exchange."

**Australia** (already confirmed per brief, re-verified incidentally): the IMF's own 1957 Annual Report independently corroborates the 1957 easing (earlier than the brief's cited Aug 1959/Dec 1959/Feb 1960 stages) — "In Australia, where import restrictions had been intensified twice in 1955 and again in 1956... the import restrictions were relaxed in several stages early in 1957" (see T0.1 file). This strengthens rather than weakens Australia's status as the working comparator: relaxation began even earlier than previously documented.

**Bonus comparators, not on the original required list ("if cheap") — and these complicate the picture:**

New Zealand — controls persisted far longer than any of the three required comparators:
> "Although the import licensing system and foreign exchange controls were supposed to be temporary, they remained in place for 50 years. While other countries introduced such measures in wartime, New Zealand was the only developed country to maintain comprehensive licence controls of manufactured imports long into peacetime." "...import restrictions remained in force, in part to ensure full employment, through the 1960s, 1970s and into the 1980s."

South Africa — controls were *tightened*, not liberalised, in the period after India's 1957 Act:
> "This changed during the apartheid era (the period from 1961 to 1993)... in 1961 the controls were extended and given a specific South African function following disrupting internal political clashes (the Sharpeville incident), and South Africa's withdrawal from the British Commonwealth... the 1961–1993 era was characterised by a comprehensive system of exchange controls embracing both current and capital account transactions."
