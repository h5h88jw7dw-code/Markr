# T2.1 — The residual No. 2 Account balance at 30 June 1957

### T2.1 — FOUND (and the finding complicates the counterfactual the brief was hoping to size)
**Route that worked:** Same RBI institutional history chapter as T1.2, "The Problems of Plenty, 1947–56" (`rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/content/PDFs/90037.pdf`), which — despite its title's end-date — extends its account of the No. 2 Account forward to its actual expiry in 1957. Full text at `primary_docs/RBI_History_Problems_of_Plenty_1947-56_FULLTEXT.md`.

**Verbatim extract:**

On the structure of the account (background, for readers unfamiliar with it):
> "The 14 August agreement did not mandate a freeze of India's sterling assets. The same object was, however, largely achieved by splitting existing balances into a No. 1 account, which was a current account, and a **blocked No. 2 account**, out of which could only be made payments for purposes such as acquiring surplus military stores, discharging pension liabilities, and financing capital outflows from India to the rest of the sterling area."

On the six-year agreement (concluded July 1949, "presented to the Indian Parliament in December 1950" per the same source) that governed the No. 2 Account through to 1957:
> "The agreement also envisaged **the immediate transfer of £310 million, representing the assets of the Reserve Bank's Issue Department, from the No. 2 account to the No. 1 account** on the condition that it would not be drawn down except in consultation with the British government. The Indian negotiators also sought agreement to discontinue the latter obligation at the end of six years. Their counterparts preferred to hold the matter over until this period had passed, but agreed to transfer any balance left in the No. 2 account at the end of six years to the No. 1 account."

**On the actual expiry — this is the load-bearing passage, and it directly supplies the date the brief asked for:**
> "**The six-year agreement was set to expire at the end of June 1957.** With the expected disappearance of the No. 2 account and the new currency reserve provisions in India, no purpose would have been served by a successor agreement. This was, in the event, the British view which was conveyed to the Indian government a year before the 1951 agreement was due to come to an end. But Britain remained keen to ensure that no restrictions were imposed on the transfer of capital from India to the United Kingdom, and in order to allay investors' fears, sought a public and authoritative declaration from the Indian government to that effect. The Indian government was not inclined to demur. Following some preliminary exercises in the Finance Ministry and with the Prime Minister's approval, **the government issued a press release announcing that the expiring agreement would not be renewed, and that the absence of a fresh agreement would neither affect India's right to draw upon its sterling balances nor those of British citizens in India to remit savings and repatriate investments. The decade-old controversy over India's sterling balances and its right to utilize them thus ended on a rather amicable note.**"

**Confidence tag:** STATED (RBI's own institutional history, directly fetched).

**Verdict against acceptance criterion:** met for the date (30 June/end-June 1957 — essentially simultaneous with the FERA permanence debate, a striking coincidence worth noting in its own right) but **not met, and importantly so, for the "counterfactual sizing" purpose the brief wanted this for.** The brief's framing (grounding gap G1: "how much of the 1956–57 gap would India's own blocked money have covered?") implicitly assumed the No. 2 Account was still meaningfully "blocked" and substantial at expiry, such that its release could be sized against the 1956–57 balance-of-payments gap. **What was actually found undercuts that framing:** the account's expiry is described as a largely *technical, mutually agreed, amicable wind-down*, explicitly linked to India's own domestic currency-reserve reform (the minimum-reserve system — see T1.7, which independently dates the relevant RBI Act amendment to 31 October 1957, i.e. four months *after* this expiry) making the whole No. 1/No. 2 split largely redundant, not to any dramatic release of a large blocked sum that then helped cover the 1956–57 gap. The bulk of the account's substance (£310 million) had already been notionally transferred to the No. 1 (current, usable) account back in the 1949–51 agreement, subject only to a consultation requirement with Britain that lapsed at the six-year mark — so the "release" at June 1957 was the lapsing of a consultation *requirement*, not the unlocking of a previously inaccessible sum of money.

**What this changes.** This is a genuine complication for any version of the essay's counterfactual that imagines a large sum of "India's own blocked money" becoming suddenly available right when needed and ask why it didn't save the day. The more accurate picture, per this source: by 1957 the sterling-balances *machinery* (No. 1/No. 2 split, consultation requirements) was winding down on a schedule agreed back in 1950–51 and was not itself the proximate cause of the 1956–57 crisis — the crisis was a Five-Year-Plan-driven current balance-of-payments deficit (already well documented via T0.1/T1.4's IMF and FRUS material), which is a different mechanism from "blocked reserves." The essay should not claim a specific rupee/sterling figure for "how much India's own blocked money would have covered" without either (a) finding the actual residual No. 2 Account balance as of the wind-down (not located in this source — it describes the *terms* of the 1949–51 transfer, not a running balance through to 1957) or (b) reframing the counterfactual away from "blocked money that could have been used" toward "the reserve-adequacy provisions that replaced it," which is the more defensible reading of what actually happened.
