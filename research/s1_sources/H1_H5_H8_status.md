## H5 — White Paper on Sterling Balances Negotiations (laid 9 August 1948)

**Status: NOT FOUND.** No digitised copy located. This is the underlying treaty document cited page-by-page by Z. H. Lari in the 12 August 1948 Constituent Assembly debate (already captured, see T1.3d), and remains the single most-wanted missing primary document behind the essay's best debate. Not indexed under an obvious title on archive.org's Gazette or Parliamentary-debates collections; may have been published as a standalone Government of India command paper rather than in the Gazette itself, which would put it in a different, unindexed collection.

**Adjacent find, not a substitute but useful context:** the Bank of England's own unpublished WWII history, Chapter 5, "India's Sterling Balances" (`bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/archive/ww/boe-1939-1945-partiii-chapterv.pdf`), fetched in full this pass and saved at `primary_docs/BoE_WWII_History_Chapter5_Indias_Sterling_Balances_FULLTEXT.md`. This is the Bank's own internal institutional account of *how* the balances accumulated in the first place (1940–46) — the war-finance mechanics (the Defence Expenditure Plan, the "equal sacrifice" debate inside the Bank and Treasury, the 40%-gold-cover statutory requirement already binding the Reserve Bank of India's Issue Department even in wartime, the various sterling-loan vesting/repatriation operations). It predates the essay's main 1948–57 period and does not cover the 1948 White Paper specifically, but it is a genuinely primary, previously-unused institutional source establishing *why* the balances existed and *how early* (1941–43) British officials were already privately debating whether India's sterling would be "at her free disposal" — Lord Keynes is quoted (1941/42 internal Bank correspondence) suggesting explicitly that India be told her balances "would not in fact be at her free disposal," a suggestion the Bank/Treasury rejected at the time in favour of publicly maintaining they would remain free "subject to the Exchange Control Regulations" — i.e. the ambiguity that later produced the 1948–57 disputes was present, internally, from the very beginning. Worth mining further if the essay wants a WWII-origins section.
