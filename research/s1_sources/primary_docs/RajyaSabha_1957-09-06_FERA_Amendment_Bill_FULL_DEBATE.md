## ⚠️ THE DECISIVE FINDING

This sitting was the essay's last plausible hiding place for a British/sterling justification of
FERA's permanence. The upper house had longer speeches, more sceptical members, and Bhupesh Gupta
(CPI) - who does in fact attack the sterling relationship in this very debate.

**The result is a clean negative, and it is now the strongest single piece of evidence in the
project**, because it is the one debate where the sterling question was *actually raised on the
floor* and the Government *answered it directly*.

### 1. The Government's stated reason for permanence — Shri B. R. Bhagat, Deputy Minister of Finance, moving the Bill

> "The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947, is a temporary Act due to expire on the 31st December,
> 1957. **It was being extended every five years in the expectation of better times, instead of being
> placed on a permanent footing straight way. India is still short of foreign exchange and I see no
> reason why this Act should not be extended without specifying a time limit.** This object is sought
> to be achieved by deleting the duration clause from section 1 of the principal Act."

**One reason, and one only: "India is still short of foreign exchange."** No Britain. No sterling
agreement. No mention of the six-year agreement's expiry on 30 June 1957 - ten weeks earlier.

Note also "**in the expectation of better times**" - the same register as Deshmukh in February 1952
("the earlier hopes of the Select Committee that world trading conditions would return to normal
... have not been fulfilled") and as TTK. This is a settled official narrative across three
ministers and eleven years: the 1947 sunset was a wager that the emergency was temporary, and 1957
is the executive declaring the wager lost.

### 2. Bhupesh Gupta DID attack the sterling relationship — and this is what he actually said

Recovered verbatim at last. Read it carefully, because it does **not** say what Track 2 or the
essay's original thesis would want it to say:

> "Then, Sir, we spend our foreign exchange in London by paying the deficit in the balance of
> payments. We were told in 1946-47 that these were the nation's assets. **The British built those
> sterling balances not through voluntary transactions as far as India is concerned. They were again
> a forced accumulation.** One should have thought that this Government would take every care to so
> utilise these sterling balances that we could undo the mischief of the British in this country and
> build up bit by bit the foundations of our country. **Even that was not done.**"

> "Now, Sir, again there is the sterling reserve for our currency. As the hon. Minister and as some
> hon. Members have said, our rupee is strong. I entirely agree that our rupee is strong. The
> currency of a country depends not on relations only but on its own internal economy. Considering
> that the rupee is strong, then, **why must be this currency reserve in sterling, in England? Many
> countries do not have such a reserve.**"

**What this is:** an attack on the *Government* for squandering the sterling balances, and a
challenge to the *sterling currency reserve* as an unnecessary colonial residue. "Forced
accumulation" and "the mischief of the British" are genuinely quotable and genuinely anti-colonial.

**What this is not:** a claim that Britain caused, compelled, or required FERA's permanence. Gupta
is arguing the Government wasted the balances and should not hold its reserve in London. He is
**criticising the Bill's context, not justifying the Bill**. The essay may quote him for the
"forced accumulation" characterisation of the wartime balances - a real find - but must not let
that quotation drift into standing for a 1957 causal claim it does not make.

### 3. The Government answered the sterling charge directly — and named the Second Plan

Bhagat, replying to Gupta:

> "My conception is that the foreign exchange crisis is not due to the fact that we have been
> squandering our resources. Nothing of the kind. We have successfully implemented the First Five
> Year Plan. We are implementing the Second Five Year Plan, and **it is industrialisation and our
> developmental needs that have compelled us to draw on our sterling balances.**"
>
> **SHRI P. D. HIMATSINGKA:** "Crisis of development."

**This is the money passage.** Directly challenged on sterling, in the upper house, ten weeks after
the six-year agreement expired, the Government's answer is *industrialisation and developmental
needs* - the Second Five Year Plan. A backbencher supplies the phrase "**crisis of development**."
Britain is not offered as an explanation even when the subject is squarely on the table.

### 4. Bonus — the ORIGINAL 1946 Statement of Objects and Reasons, quoted on the floor

Shri Amolakh Chand (Uttar Pradesh) narrates the Act's history and quotes the Finance Minister's 1946
Statement of Objects and Reasons directly:

> "**The shortage of foreign exchange is likely to continue in view of the disruption of the internal
> economy** ... [and] the general interests of the country. Also **the adherence of India to the
> International Monetary Fund requires her to take certain [powers over] foreign exchange in order
> to fulfil the obligations of membership. Legislation is therefore necessary**"

He also dates the machinery's origin: "under the Defence of India Rules for the first time the
question of foreign exchange arose in India. Under the Defence of India Rules of **1939** foreign
exchange was to be regulated, and it was to be regulated in non-sterling areas. The Defence of India
Rules expired in March, 1946."

**The two reasons in the 1946 SOR are the same two Deshmukh gave in 1952 and Bhagat gives in 1957:
foreign exchange shortage, and IMF membership obligations.** Eleven years, three ministers, one
consistent pair of reasons - and Britain is in none of them.

## Confidence and verdict

**Confidence tag: STATED** throughout - the official parliamentary record of the sitting, ministers'
and members' own words.

**Verdict:** the essay's original thesis - that British/sterling pressure caused FERA's permanence -
now fails its **last available test**. This is the eighth independent 1956-57 source to give
non-British reasons, and uniquely it is the one where the sterling question was *raised on the floor
by a hostile member* and *answered by the Government on the record*. There is no remaining
substantial primary document in which the claim could still be hiding.

**What the essay gains instead:**
1. A clean, quotable ministerial statement of the actual reason ("India is still short of foreign
   exchange"), from the debate that enacted permanence in the second chamber.
2. Bhupesh Gupta's "**forced accumulation**" and "**the mischief of the British**" - excellent
   anti-colonial material for Act I, about the *wartime* balances, correctly scoped.
3. Gupta's challenge to the sterling currency reserve ("why must be this currency reserve in
   sterling, in England?"), which is a real and underused angle: the *reserve composition* question,
   distinct from the *permanence* question.
4. "**Crisis of development**" - Himatsingka's phrase, and a precise three-word summary of what the
   Government thought was happening.

## RESOLUTION REGARDING INTER- NATIONAL CONVENTION FOR THE PROTECTION OF CULTURAL PRO- PERTY IN THE EVENT OF ARMED CONFLICT

THE MINISTER OF STATE IN THE
MINISTRY OF EDUCATION AND
SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH (DR. K. L.
SHRIMALI): Sir, I beg to move Gift following
Resolution:

* * *

"This House approves the Convention
for the protection of cultural property in the
event of armed conflict, as passed at the
Hague on the 14th May, 1954, and signed
by the representatives of the Government
of India and of the Governments of certain
other countries, and is of the opinion that
the said Convention should be ratified by
the Government of India."

Sir, in moving this Resolution I should
briefly like to inform the Hou-e about the
origin of this Convention and also some of its
special features. During the last two wars,
great damage was done to the cultural
properties. It was realised by UNESCO that
cultural property does not belong to any
particular nation but belongs to the mankind
as a whole. Many atrocities are performed
during the war, but to my mind, the greatest
atrocity that is performed is the destruction _ol_
cultural properties. Humanity goes down very
low during an armed conflict. We lose hold of
all the essential good values of life but
nothing is more ravaging, nothing is more
destructive of human values and human
culture than the destruction of the cultural
properties. Having realised this great truth,
the UNESCO called a Conference at the
Hague in 1954. This Conference was attended
by the several nations including India and a
Convention was drawn up and adopted.
Several nations were signatories to this
Convention including India. Now the
Convention has to be ratified.

This document has three parts. There is the
actual Convention for the protection of
cultural property in the event of armed
conflict. Then there are regulations for the
execution of the said Convention and a
Protocol to the Convention for the protection
of cultural property in the event of armed
conflicts.

The cultural property, according to Article
1, will cover:

(a) All movable or Immovable property of
great importance to the cul-

tural heritage of every people, such as
monuments of architecture, art or history,
whether religious or secular, archaeological
sites; groups of buildings which, as a whole,
are of historical or artistic interest; works of
art, manuscripts, books and other objects of
artistic, historical or archaeological interest; as
well as scientific collections and important
collections of books or archives or of
reproductions of the property defined above;

(b) Buildings whose main and effective
purpose is to preserve or exhibit the
