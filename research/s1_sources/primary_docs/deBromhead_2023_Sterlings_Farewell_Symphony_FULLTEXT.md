# Sterling’s Farewell Symphony: The End of the Sterling Area Revisited

## Abstract

When and why did Sterling Area countries stop holding sterling as the majority of their 
FX reserves? This paper takes a comparative approach to examine the relative 
importance of various determinants of adherence to sterling in its declining years as an 
international currency. Using an original cross-national panel dataset covering the
period 1965 to 1979, we conduct survival analysis which systematically evaluates a 
comprehensive set of economic and political factors, at the country level as well as in 
international relations, about when and why countries chose to diversify their reserves 
away from sterling. Our results highlight the significance of international transactional
factors in influencing adherence to sterling,  while the effects of  British  geopolitical
retrenchment, Commonwealth  cultural ties, and distributional issues were more 
ambiguous and sensitive to local conditions. We also find that domestic political and 
historical factors, such as democracy and imperial legacy, played a role in sterling’s
international  unravelling. Finally, we use our results to examine the experience of 
individual sterling countries and their decisions to diversify.

**Key words:** The Sterling Area; international monetary regime; international 
currency; sterling; decline; disintegration

JEL Codes: N10, F02, F22, F33

## 1 Introduction

The sound of the orchestra quietens in the Final Movement of Haydn’s ‘Farewell’
Symphony as each musician, in turn, stops playing, snuffs out the candle on their music 
stand, and leaves. At the end, only two violins remain.

In this paper, we find that countries of the postwar Sterling Area¹ followed a similar pattern 
when diversifying their international reserves away from sterling. However, they each, in 
turn, dropped sterling as their majority foreign exchange (FX) holding, not only in response 
to the waving of a British conductor’s baton, but for reasons peculiar to their own 
circumstances and requirements. Here we examine this country variation in the timing of 
reserve policy transition, and the ‘demand-side’ country-specific factors – economic and 
political, international and domestic – that contributed to international sterling’s post-
1965 ‘Farewell’.

as the Sterling Area, there were inevitably many other factors behind diversification. We
therefore derive the changing survival probabilities of individual sterling countries over 
time, and identify early- and late-leaving countries relative to the predictions of our model

observed transition timings as discrepancies, we exploit this variance to examine individual 
countries’ transitions from sterling. The analysis of these country details confirms the 
multidimensional nature of sterling diversification with war, diplomatic relations, local 
pull-factors, economic weakness and country variations in reserve management all playing 
a role.

historical context for the Sterling Area focusing on the supply side. Importantly, we use this 
Section also to present our findings for the dependent variable, mapping the transition of 
sterling countries away from majority sterling FX holdings. In Section 3 we identify 
conjectures, derived from various literatures, about what might explain countries’ 
diversification from sterling. Section 4 presents and interprets the empirical results of the 
duration model. Section 5 examines divergences between the model’s survival predictions 
and the actual years of transition, finding reasons for these divergences through historical 
sources. A final Section concludes.

## 2 The End of the Sterling Area in Historical Context

The standard economic model explaining aggregate currency shares in international 
9
reserves attributes importance to three supply-side variables affecting currency demand.
Firstly, there are network externalities, the idea that an issuer’s economic size or financial 
depth is what attracts investors, and that widespread take-up further increases demand for 
the currency. The network effects can create a tipping point, when one leading currency is 
overtaken by another, and investors switch allegiance. Secondly, there is policy credibility, 
proxied by the risk-return performance of the exchange rate. Thirdly, there are persistence 
or inertia effects slowing the rate of change, produced by factors such as habit and a lack of 
alternatives.

In the aggregate, the decline of sterling as an international currency during the twentieth 
century has been well documented using the above framework. It was during the 1920s that 
the US dollar first overtook sterling as the world’s leading currency.<sup>10</sup>

recoveries, confidence in sterling gradually eroded, and British economic weakness

share of FX reserves.  After 1945, the UK was weak relative to the USA in scale and 
credibility, and it was mainly forms of inertia that acted to slow sterling’s decline.<sup>12</sup>

Defining the Sterling Area is not straightforward. It is commonly defined as a list of 
countries enjoying privileged status in British exchange control legislation. When wartime 
exchange control on sterling payments by UK residents to non-residents was introduced in 
September 1939, payments to certain countries with close British connections were 
exempted from the regulation. ‘In this way the Sterling Area became a legal entity, an area 
inside which payment in sterling was unrestricted’.<sup>18</sup>
After the war, the controls and 
privilege continued, and following the Exchange Control Act 1947, the official shorthand 
became that Sterling Area countries were listed as ‘scheduled territories’ enjoying the 
exemption. In this way, exchange control ‘provided the condition for the development of 
the Sterling Area as an exclusive club whose members regarded each other as different from 
outsiders’.<sup>19</sup>
Accordingly the end of the Sterling Area is conventionally dated at June 1972, 
when the list of scheduled territories was reduced to ‘the British Isles and Ireland’ ‘as a 
20
footnote to the floating of the pound’.

the scheduled territories. Firstly, British officials in June 1972 were clear, both internally 
and publicly, that they were not ending the Sterling Area. Rather, this was the

## 5 Model of ‘Best Fit’ and Survival Probabilities Discussion

The overarching goal of our duration model analysis is to identify important systematic 
drivers of adherence to sterling.  To this end, model 14 in Table 3 brings the variables 
together that ‘best fit’ our data based on AIC and BIC minimization. Overall, this generates
a plausible multidimensional picture of the push- and pull-factors driving reserve policies 
across countries.  The expected trade-related transactional factors are included in the 
model, but so too are politics, specifically democracy, the Sterling Agreements, and colonial 
legacy.

While these patterns in the panel data highlight the key systemic drivers of Sterling Area
disintegration,  it is also important to understand the nuanced ways in which different 
factors combined to influence the choices of individual members. Here, our dynamic model

Though it is hard to define precisely when a country should transition away from majority 
sterling FX reserves, following the approach in Wandschneider, it is possible to argue that

Finally, as a wealthy country, Australia’s ‘late’ transition was clearly different from that of 
the less developed countries. Its survival probability was already less than 50% in 1969, yet 
transition only occurred in 1972. A plausible explanation is an outsized delaying effect from 
adherence to its Sterling Agreement (the MSP was reduced by 10% in 1971).

In summary, deviations from the predictions of our empirical model are well explained by
idiosyncratic factors outside the model including war, diplomatic relationships, economic 
weakness, and local pull-factors, such as Singapore’s influence on Malaysia. These 
individual country experiences support our conclusions about the multidimensional 
drivers of policy change, and show a local  role for geopolitics, culture, and financial 
dependence, alongside conventional economic considerations.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a novel perspective on the end of sterling’s international 
role, achieved by giving sustained attention to sterling’s share of national FX reserves. This 
perspective rests not simply on a reconsideration of the historical evidence and new source 
materials. It rests, too, on a shift in the focus of historical analysis from the formal juridical 
definition of the end of the Sterling Area, based on exchange controls and highly visible 
pegging decisions, to the more veiled and differentiated process of reserve diversification.
Formal endings matter for understanding the end of the Sterling Area, but so too do the 
unwinding of residual informal relationships.

Overall, our interrogation of economic factors suggests that domestic income level effects 
mattered more than performance dynamics for adherence to sterling, while international transactional forces exerted more influence than portfolio considerations in reserve policy 
changes. There is support for access arguments in trade, but the evidence for capital is
inconclusive. Meanwhile,  there is no statistical evidence that  currency holders  were
‘trapped’ by risks of economic loss or the costs of switching. 
The place of international geopolitical factors is ambiguous.  For some countries facing 
security uncertainties, such as Kuwait and Pakistan, withdrawal of British support seemed 
to accelerate a search for alternative monetary arrangements. The pull of US financial 
assistance also demonstrates that international currencies can be undercut by powerful 
strategic rivals. But our econometric analysis does not support the inference that reserve
policy towards sterling was systematically associated with geopolitical considerations. 
Nor can we conclude that Commonwealth bonds of loyalty, friendship, and cultural 
attachment extended sterling adherence. This null finding perhaps indicates that while 
some countries were culturally aligned with Britain, others wanted to rid themselves of 
their old imperial master. British officials may themselves have underestimated the latter 
sentiment, as seen in the tortuous  Sterling Agreements negotiations of 1968 and 
subsequent exits from them.
Nonetheless,  it seems that  there remains a role for politics in the process of monetary
disintegration.  In the case of the Sterling Area, this manifested itself not so much in 
distributional  asymmetries  and conflicts about relative contributions, gains and losses. 
Rather, it was to be found partly  in domestic politics, with democracy serving to delay 
transitions from sterling – more research being needed to better understand this channel.
The international political order – in the form of structures of dependence and past colonial relationships – also exerted an identifiable influence on countries’ transitions away from 
sterling.

What lessons does this historical survey offer regarding the determinants of international 
currency choice or how international currency transitions occur? Supply-side models of 
international currency choice, focused on an issuer’s economic size and network effects,
feature prominently in the literature.<sup>117</sup>
Models that emphasise demand-side factors feature 
less, although the arguments of Eichengreen and others, demonstrating that the demandside matters too, offer a valuable alternative perspective.<sup>118</sup>
We interpret our findings in 
relation to the Sterling Area from this demand-side viewpoint.

Theoretical positions aside, this paper reminds us that the reality of international currency 
choice is complex, multidimensional, and often difficult to predict. Flandreau, for instance, 
found the decline of nineteenth-century bimetallism to have been the result of irrational 
actions, and chaotic and contingent events.<sup>119</sup>
Similarly, the reserve transitions of Sterling 
Area countries in the 1960s-70s did not accord with simple explanations. They reveal a 
heterogeneous, contingent, demand-side story. The picture that emerges is 
multidimensional, and much explanation lies qualitatively in contingencies outside the 
systematic dimensions outlined by the formal explanatory model, such as the impact of 
local events. Understanding when and why the music stops requires following the 
movements of individual players, not just the conductor.
