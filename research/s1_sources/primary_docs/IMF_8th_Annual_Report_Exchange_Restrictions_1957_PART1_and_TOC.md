## I. Introduction

As the Fund completes its eleventh year of operation, it is able to report further progress in the relaxation of exchange restrictions, although the progress in the past year was not at the same rate as in preceding years. The process of freeing trade and payments was continued in spite of a reduction in the international reserves of certain important countries.

In the Annual Report of the Executive Directors published in 1956, it was observed that \"foreign exchange restrictions impose a less serious obstacle to international commerce today than at any time since the outbreak of World War II.\" Restrictions—and these include restrictions on trade as well as on payments—have been relaxed still further since that observation was made. At the present time, therefore, the Fund can report that further progress has been made toward its objective of establishing a fully multilateral system of payments. Against this it is to be noted that a large majority of Fund members still retain exchange control—although with widely differing degrees of restrictiveness in its application—and that in many instances it is not possible to predict an early establishment of a truly multilateral payments regime.

At present, member countries, so far as their restrictive practices are concerned, can be classified broadly as follows: In Central and North America there are eleven countries with convertible currencies that do not avail themselves of the transitional arrangements of Article XIV of the Fund Agreement and which maintain minimal exchange restrictions, if any. In Western Europe, some countries have so liberalized trade and payments that it appears they would be able, without any very significant readjustment, to participate in a general move to establish a regime of full convertibility. Gradually approaching this position are the United Kingdom and a number of other Western European and Sterling Area countries, in which stable unitary exchange rates predominate but restrictions are applied to limit certain imports, with a lessening but still significant distinction between currencies of payment—particularly between dollars and other currencies. Nonresident holders of currencies of the countries in this group are generally able to obtain dollars at rates close to parity. Outside Europe and North and Central America, several countries, of which Lebanon, Peru, and Thailand are examples, have for some time maintained liberal nondiscriminatory import and payment policies which come very near to full convertibility. Finally, there are countries that retain relatively elaborate systems of exchange control, including complex systems of multiple rates and bilateral arrangements, and often distinguish between commodities as well as between countries and currencies of payment. For these countries, considerable further progress would have to be made before participation in a general move to a liberal and convertible regime could be contemplated.

The present Report covers the period since the end of April 1956. Part I gives a brief general survey of developments in exchange restrictions throughout the world. Part II describes the main features of each member's restrictive system as at the end of 1956, and includes brief descriptions of the exchange systems of member countries not availing themselves of the transitional arrangements. In addition, surveys of certain nonmember countries are included. Important changes made in the early part of 1957 are also reported.

## II. Recent Developments in Exchange Restrictions

## General Developments

The trend toward reduction in exchange restrictions, which has continued for some years, has brought a considerable degree of freedom to the arrangements governing international trade and payments. For some countries, the remaining steps will be greater freedom of capital transfers and the complete abolition of exchange restrictions, together with acceptance of full convertibility. It is noteworthy that the process of relaxation, although slowed down by the international tensions which developed during 1956, was not halted, but continued with certain exceptions during the year. This further progress was attained for the most part by a continuation of trends discernible in previous years: areas of transferability were further widened, bilateral arrangements were liberalized, lists of goods which could be imported free of quotas were further extended, regulations governing payments abroad were further simplified. On the other hand, multiple exchange rate systems in various countries continued without fundamental change, with the exception of Bolivia, which unified its exchange rate structure.

A few European countries increased restrictions during the past twelve months, but these were exceptional cases; most European countries made appreciable progress toward a more liberal system of trade and payments. Japan and Argentina achieved an appreciable amount of liberalization in their payments systems by substituting transferable payments arrangements for bilateral arrangements.

In other countries also some progress was achieved, but the direction of developments was not so clearly defined. The modification of bilateral arrangements made possible by a greater degree of transferability of currencies affected a good many countries. Some also achieved further reductions in restrictions by increased use of free exchange markets. On the other hand, a few countries met internal and external difficulties by intensifying their exchange restrictions.

A discernible trend, in part continued from previous years, was that of seeking to reduce restrictions on a regional basis as a method of progressing toward wider systems of multilateral trade and payments. In addition to the continuing efforts of OEEC countries to liberalize trade within that area, a new regime of trade and payments was established between Argentina and eleven European countries (the \"Paris

Club\");1 and three more European countries (Austria, France, and Italy) joined the arrangements which had been set up in the previous year under which Brazil placed its transactions with a number of European countries on a multilateral basis (the \"Hague Club\").2

Another regional development is the signing of a treaty by the governments of six continental European countries establishing a European Economic Community linked by a common market.3 There have also been discussions within the OEEC on the establishment of a wider free trade area. The Scandinavian countries have continued negotiations for a customs union. Regional programs among groups of Latin American countries have also been discussed, involving in one case a customs union among countries of Central America and in the other a multilateral payments arrangement among a group of countries in South America.

The past year has been especially noteworthy for the efforts made by member countries, including some faced with balance of payments difficulties, to find more fundamental remedies for their problems, and thus to avoid any intensification of exchange restrictions. There has been a growing awareness of the need to strengthen fiscal measures as well as monetary measures in order to fortify the internal economy, and to curb excessive demand so as to relieve pressure on the balance of payments and to reduce the need for exchange restrictions.

Another measure that countries have taken to avoid the imposition of exchange restrictions has been to mobilize sufficient foreign exchange reserves to withstand temporary pressure upon their currencies. An important example in the past year was the decision of the United Kingdom to meet a sudden crisis of confidence in sterling in other ways than by an intensification of exchange restrictions. The United Kingdom in December 1956 obtained from the Fund a drawing of $561,470,000 and a stand-by arrangement for $738,530,000, to meet the pressure on its currency. The fact that the United Kingdom avoided a reimposition of trade and payments restrictions, played an important part in sustaining the momentum toward continued liberalization of payments regimes in other countries throughout the world.

There was a further extension of the transferability of the currencies of the non-dollar world, on which the reduction of much discrimination and bilateralism depends. The reappearance of seriously inconsistent cross rates was avoided, so that in those countries where free exchange markets exist trade has not been distorted by disorderly exchange rate quotations. Following upon the payments difficulties encountered during the past year, particularly by the United Kingdom, rates for transferable, but inconvertible, currencies in free markets fell somewhat; however, the discounts remained generally within a range of 1¾ per cent of parity, and thus not only was a resurgence of so-called commodity shunting avoided, but there were no serious disturbances to payments.

## Increase of Transferability

Exchange discrimination among non-dollar countries was further reduced during the year by a continued extension of facilities for transferring their currencies, such as had been established earlier for sterling and the deutsche mark. Belgium-Luxembourg, Italy, the Netherlands, and Sweden all established transferable currency areas for their currencies. The Federal Republic of Germany added several countries to the transferable DM area, usually in the process of revising bilateral agreements in such a manner as to eliminate bilateral restrictions on payments with new participants in the transferable DM area. The Western European countries have, with few exceptions, given to residents of all non-dollar countries the facility of exchanging balances on their nonresident accounts into other currencies in the Western European multilateral arbitrage arrangement. Through the facilities thus granted and the use of the free exchange markets for transferable sterling, it is generally feasible for nonresident holders of a number of European currencies to convert them into dollars at a fairly slight discount. Another important development was the creation of a new system of payments between Argentina and several European countries, as mentioned above. These particular arrangements provide for the interchangeability by the Argentine authorities of the currencies of the European countries concerned, so that discrimination among them respecting Argentine trade and payments is to a large extent eliminated.

Finally, the multilateral exchange arbitrage arrangement operating in Europe (see Fifth Annual Report on Exchange Restrictions, pages 15-16) was extended to include dealings in Austrian schillings and transactions with Austrian banks. During the year, the limit for forward exchange transactions under this arrangement was extended from three months to six months (with the exception of transactions in French francs or with banks in France). Most of the participating countries have extended similar facilities to banks in Finland.

## Dollar Discrimination

Continued progress was made toward liberalization of dollar imports, particularly by continental European countries. Austria, the Federal Republic of Germany, Italy, Norway, Sweden, and France announced increased liberalization of such imports. Ceylon added several commodities to its dollar import list, with the result that its import discrimination against the dollar area has virtually ended. In these and other countries, a lessening of dollar discrimination has also occurred through increased liberalization in the administrative application of trade and exchange controls.

## Bilateral Arrangements

In June 1955, the Fund issued an appeal to its members to reduce and to eliminate as rapidly as practicable reliance on bilateralism (see Appendix I of the Annual Report of the Executive Directors, 1955). Some progress on this account was recorded in the Seventh Annual Report on Exchange Restrictions (page 5), and the trend toward the elimination of bilateralism gathered momentum during 1956. Partly as a result of the increased facilities for transferring currencies, many bilateral payments agreements were terminated and others were modified so as to eliminate the requirement that payments be conducted only on a bilateral basis. Substantial further progress was achieved by various countries which had already begun in previous years to dismantle their bilateral payments agreements, and other countries joined in this movement. A number of countries which, hitherto, had made considerable use of bilateral agreements adopted policies that enabled them to move away from bilateralism. Argentina modified its bilateral payments relations with eleven European countries when the \"Paris Club\" arrangements came into effect and also agreed to conduct payments with Japan on a sterling basis. The Federal Republic of Germany abolished (or modified, as indicated above) nine bilateral payments agreements in 1956 and two more in the early months of 1957. Countries taking similar action with respect to five or more agreements include Chile, France, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, and Uruguay. Relatively few member countries entered into new agreements during the year, and the total number of discriminatory bilateral agreements in force declined substantially. While progress in the formal elimination of bilateral trade agreements was less striking, the trend away from specific licensing commitments under such agreements continued.

## Multiple Currency Practices

Progress in eliminating or decreasing multiple currency practices was less marked in the period under review than in the preceding years. Last year's Report mentioned (page 5) that the payments systems of Chile and Paraguay were greatly simplified. Bolivia can now be added to this group, since in December 1956 it put into effect widespread changes in its exchange system, replacing a multiple exchange rate system by a single fluctuating rate for all transactions. Import licensing was abolished and a stabilization program that included fiscal, monetary, and other measures was adopted.

Some other countries introduced new free markets or added to the number and kinds of transactions which could be conducted in existing free markets, although generally these changes were of minor importance in their exchange systems. On various occasions during the year, Argentina shifted specified imports and minor exports from the official exchange market to the free market. Viet-Nam established a separate exchange market for certain invisibles and capital transactions. Ecuador unified two free markets by the withdrawal of official support for the rates in its controlled free market.

A few countries made alterations in their exchange rate systems which reduced the number of existing rates and hence gave rise to a somewhat simpler rate structure. Afghanistan reduced the number of its fixed exchange rates from three to two. China (Taiwan) merged separate rates for certain exports with other rates prevailing in its rate structure. Iceland abolished the use of import certificates and levied a tax of 16 per cent on sales of exchange for imports and invisibles, with some exceptions.

On the other hand, some countries put into effect new multiple rates or made increased use of existing multiple currency practices. In Turkey, the fluctuating rates for transactions under the deblockage system were replaced by a fixed rate, the use of which was extended to cover a limited number of invisibles. In Israel, a system of export premiums for some industrial exports paid for in U.S. dollars and certain other currencies came into effect. Chile introduced a tax of 1 per cent on foreign exchange payments and later increased this tax to 5 per cent. Turkey imposed a tax of 40 per cent on foreign exchange sold at the official rate for the import of a wide range of commodities. France introduced a tax on the sale of foreign exchange to French residents for travel. Some countries having complex systems of multiple exchange rates made changes in the operation of those systems during the year, without, however, achieving notable progress in the direc- tion of simplifying their arrangements or freeing trade and payments. Brazil made several changes in export classifications and in export bonuses. Indonesia introduced a system of negotiable export incentive certificates, by which exporters of most goods receive bonuses, while importers of specified commodities must have these certificates in order to obtain the exchange to pay for imports. This alteration was accompanied by a modification of the levies on imports and resulted in an increased number of effective import rates. Uruguay introduced a \"free\" certificate market for imports and exports; eleven groups of exports were established for nonmanufactured export products, each group receiving a \"mixing\" rate; and \"mixing\" rates were also established for exports of manufactured goods.<sup>4</sup>

## Other Changes

As indicated above, the progress achieved during the year was in part a result of numerous individual relaxations which were not always of any great significance in themselves. This is especially true of the continued efforts to simplify and make less burdensome the restrictions which it was thought necessary to retain and the exchange control machinery for the enforcement of these restrictions. The moves made in this field are recorded in detail in Part II of this Report, but a few examples are given below.

Several countries, including China (Taiwan), France, the Federal Republic of Germany, India, Iran, and the United Kingdom, enlarged the powers delegated to commercial banks for deciding on exchange applications, or increased the number of banks authorized to exercise such powers, or generally eased the exchange control requirements. Argentina and Japan enlarged the list of goods licensed automatically. Some countries, such as the Federal Republic of Germany and Italy, raised the limits below which imports are permitted without any licensing formalities. Lists of goods that could be imported free of quantitative restriction from all countries or from specified areas were enlarged by Austria, Burma, Ceylon, France, the Federal Republic of Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom.

## III. Summary

The developments in the field of restrictions during the period under review may be summarized as follows:

1. In general, there was continued progress in easing restrictions and lessening discrimination, although some countries faced with balance of payments difficulties imposed somewhat stiffer trade and payments restrictions. Progress in dismantling the machinery of exchange control was not as marked as progress in relaxing restrictions.

During the year, there was a growing reliance on the Fund as a means of mobilizing sufficient reserves in order to avoid intensifying restrictions. The general tendency was to continue to apply the more fundamental remedies arising from flexible monetary and fiscal policies, rather than to resort to intensified restrictions in order to solve balance of payments problems.

There was no fundamental change in the use made of multiple currency practices.

2. There was a substantial decline in bilateralism, as revealed both in a reduction in the number of bilateral payments agreements and in less restrictive means of conducting payments under existing agreements.

3. The movement toward transferable or limited multilateral arrangements, noted in the Seventh Annual Report on Exchange Restrictions, was continued during the past year. Further progress was made in enlarging the areas of transferability of several non-dollar currencies. A further reduction in discrimination within the non-dollar world was also achieved by the extension of lists of goods not subject to quantitative restriction. There were some substantial moves to reduce discrimination against the dollar area, particularly by continental European countries.

When consideration is given to the payments difficulties encountered by several large countries during the period under review, it is satisfactory to be able to note that progress toward the establishment of a freer multilateral system of trade and payments was maintained and that no very serious setbacks occurred.

# Explanatory Note

Member countries maintaining restrictions on payments and transfers for current international transactions under Article XIV, Section 2, of the Fund Agreement are required to consult with the Fund each year as to the further retention of those restrictions. The Fund has now completed its fifth series of annual consultations. These consultations, which have taken place either in the member country or in Washington, have enabled the Fund to maintain direct and close contacts with members and have provided an opportunity for discussion of the economic and financial problems which give rise to restrictive and discriminatory practices and of the possibilities of further relaxation.

Since the last Report was written, no additional countries have ceased to avail themselves of the postwar transitional period arrangements of Article XIV. Of the 60 members of the Fund, 49 have an obligation to consult under Article XIV, including 2 members which have joined the Fund since the last Report was issued.

The following surveys of individual exchange systems of various countries are presented in three groups. The first comprises member countries which, for the most part, maintain their existing restrictions under the postwar transitional period arrangements of Article XIV. In some of these countries, however, restrictions to which the Fund has given approval under Article VIII of the Agreement may also be in operation. The second group comprises member countries which do not apply any restrictions under Article XIV; they are referred to as \"Article VIII countries.\" The third group comprises certain countries which, at the time this Report was written, were not members of the Fund.

The surveys relate generally to the exchange systems as at December 31, 1956. For a few countries, however, the surveys relate to the exchange systems at a later date or include references to significant developments which took place in early 1957. A standardized framework has been employed in drafting these surveys: each system is described under certain headings and a final section under each country lists chronologically the more significant changes which took place in the restrictive system during 1956. Notes on the subject matter under the descriptive headings used are given in the Explanatory Note to Part II of the Sixth Annual Report on Exchange Restrictions (pages 21-24), published in 1955.
