# T2 Evidence Comb — Findings

> # VERDICT: Track 2's central mechanism claim is DEAD. Do not attempt to rescue it.
>
> **Track 2 claims no import licence functioned without an FERA-gated foreign exchange release behind it — that FERA was the "chief enabler" bottleneck standing behind the whole licensing apparatus. The primary regulatory text says the opposite, in terms, and so does every independent source checked.** The RBI's own *Exchange Control Manual* — the governing instrument for authorised dealers — provides that exchange for licensed imports "may be made by authorised dealers **without approval of Reserve Bank**." The RBI's official history dates the mechanism to **May 1940** and explains *why* there was no second gate: foreign exchange was allotted **upstream**, by an inter-departmental committee, and "**on the basis of these allotments import licences can be issued**." The licence *was* the exchange decision. There was never a second one behind it. Target 0.2's narrower rescue — capital goods as a specially FERA-gated category — also fails: the only RBI approvals attaching to capital goods concern **financing structure** (deferred payment, suppliers' credit, financial lease), not the exchange release for the import.
>
> **The one thing in this run that matters most is Finding 6 — and it is a Classic-side win, not a Track 2 rescue.** The RBI's official history states that personal-travel foreign exchange amounts "**varied according to the overall foreign exchange position**." That wires the Basic Travel Quota's near-zero ceiling directly to the reserve constraint Classic already proves via Locks A and B — giving Classic a human-scale consequence on its own mechanism, with no need to borrow Track 2's framing at all. Finding 7 is the structural correction that follows: FERA's genuine discretionary gate operated on **persons** (travel, remittances, emigration), not on **trade**. That is a smaller claim than Track 2's, and unlike Track 2's, it is true.

## TIER 0 — the mechanism question

### Finding 1 — The primary regulatory text says exchange release for licensed imports is made by banks WITHOUT RBI approval. Track 2's central claim is killed.
**Serves: Track 2 (kills it)** — Grade **A**

**Source:** Reserve Bank of India, *Exchange Control Manual*, Chapter 7 ("Import of Goods, Currency etc., Merchanting Trade and Other Related Matters"), Part A, paragraph **7A.1(i)**. Retrieved in full from eximguru's reproduction of the ECM text:
`https://www.eximguru.com/exim/reserve-bank/foreign-exchange-control-manual/chapter-7-import-of-goods-currency-etc-merchanting-trade-and-other-related-matters.aspx`

**Verbatim:**
> "Import trade is regulated by the office of the Director General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) and its regional offices functioning under the Ministry of Commerce, Government of India. Policies and procedures for import are announced by the DGFT. **Sale of foreign exchange or rupee transfer to non-resident account towards payment for import of goods into India from any foreign country, except Nepal and Bhutan, permitted under the prevailing import trade control policy may be made by authorised dealers without approval of Reserve Bank** subject to the conditions set out in subsequent paragraphs."

And on the operative document, at 7A.2:
> "Authorised dealers should not open letters of credit or allow remittances for the import of goods included in the negative lists **unless the importer submits a licence marked 'For Exchange Control Purposes'**."

**Confidence: STATED** — for the period of this ECM edition. **Dating caveat, stated explicitly:** this edition references the Export and Import Policy (1992–97), the Research and Development Cess Act 1986, and the Technology Development Board Act 1995, so it is a **mid-to-late 1990s edition**. Under the brief's later-period rule, this establishes the mechanism **as of the 1990s** as STATED; it does **not** by itself establish 1947–57. Findings 2 and 3 supply the period-correct evidence, and they agree with it.

**What it does to the claim:** kills the general form of Track 2's mechanism claim outright. The governing instrument for authorised dealers states the default is release *without* RBI approval, conditioned on the licence. The licence is a **document to be produced**, not an application to a second decision-maker. This is exactly the "(a) automatic" limb of Target 0.1's binary, in the primary text.

### Finding 2 — The period-correct source: exchange "could only be sold to importers holding import licences," from May 1940.
**Serves: Track 2 (kills it) / Combined** — Grade **A**

**Source:** *History of the Reserve Bank of India*, Volume 1 (RBI's own official history, 915pp), Chapter 20, "Exchange Control and Management," section "Import Control," p. ~363. Full text retrieved from `https://dokumen.pub/the-reserve-bank-of-india-1-817596247x.html`

**Verbatim:**
> "**Import Control:** The main object of exchange control, as mentioned earlier, was to conserve the foreign currency resources of the sterling area as a whole. This was achieved, first, **through import control, established in May 1940, whereby exchange could only be sold to importers holding import licences issued by the Import Controllers situated at each of the major ports.** The issue of import licences was determined to a considerable extent by the availability of shipping space, although in many cases, licences were refused on the ground that the currency of the supplying country was 'difficult'."

And, dating the "exchange control copy" to the essay's own window:
> "As already mentioned, **from September 1, 1947**, the conditions applicable to remittances against imports from non-sterling countries were extended to remittances in respect of imports from the sterling area; in other words, **duplicate copies of import licences and Customs bills of entry had to be issued for exchange control purposes**, in regard to sterling area imports also, as from that date. This brought in its wake an enormous increase in licensing work."

**Confidence: STATED** for the 1940–51 period (RBI's official history describing its own administration, with dates given in the source itself — so this is *not* backward extrapolation).

**What it does to the claim:** this is the period-correct confirmation, and it independently corroborates the Balachandran passage that prompted this brief. Note what the grammar does: exchange "could only be sold to importers **holding** import licences." Holding, not applying again. The licence is the qualifying condition, and the sale follows from holding it. It also supplies the **origin date (May 1940)** and the date the exchange-control duplicate copy was extended to sterling-area imports (**1 September 1947**) — both of which the brief's later-period rule required before any backward reasoning, and both of which the source states itself.

### Finding 3 — Why there was no second gate: the foreign exchange was allotted BEFORE the licence was issued. The licence WAS the exchange decision.
**Serves: Track 2 (kills it, and explains why) / Combined** — Grade **A**

**Source:** *History of the Reserve Bank of India*, Vol. 1, Ch. 20, pp. ~646–47 — Governor Sir Chintaman Deshmukh's own proposal for quantitative licensing, quoted directly in the official history.

**Verbatim** (the Governor, setting out the tasks of the proposed allocation committee):
> "The first action of this Committee must be to make an estimate of foreign exchange resources both actual and prospective... Once this is done, **the Committee must fix the amount of foreign exchange that can be allotted for different categories of imports — food, fertilisers, capital goods, consumer goods, petroleum products, raw materials such as cotton and wool, and miscellaneous commodities, and on the basis of these allotments import licences can be issued.**"

Context, from the same passage — this is Deshmukh arguing the shift from qualitative to quantitative licensing:
> "the only practical method by which import control could be administered in the future was to constitute a standing departmental policy committee as in the U.K. or a Foreign Exchange Control Board as in Canada on which representatives of the spending departments of Government (e.g., Industries and Supplies, and Food), the Commerce Department and the Reserve Bank met under the chairmanship of the Finance Department **to allocate the available foreign exchange, including sterling, among competing demands. In other words, the existing qualitative licensing had to give way to a system of quantitative licensing.**"

The official history then confirms this was adopted, for the half-year July–December 1947:
> "Thus, the Governor's suggestion for quantitative licensing of imports had been accepted by the Government fully. **Individual import licences also came to be issued thereafter with the c.i.f. value as the limiting factor in all cases.**"

**Confidence: STATED** (RBI official history, quoting its own Governor, with the policy change dated to 1946–47 in the source).

**What it does to the claim:** this is the finding that makes the kill *structural* rather than merely evidential. Track 2 imagines a sequence — licence issued, then FERA gate decides whether exchange is released. The actual sequence is the reverse: **foreign exchange was allocated first, by category, by an inter-departmental committee chaired by Finance; import licences were then issued against those allotments, denominated in c.i.f. value.** Exchange rationing did not sit *behind* the licence as a second gate; it sat *inside* the licence, upstream, as the thing that determined whether the licence existed at all and for how much. There is no room in this architecture for the bottleneck Track 2 needs, because the exchange decision and the licensing decision are the same decision, taken at the same moment, by the same body — and that body was **not** the RBI acting under FERA. It was a Finance-chaired committee, with the RBI as one participant among several.

**Corollary worth stating in the essay:** this also means the exchange constraint was *more* determinative of licensing than Track 2 claims, not less — but it operated through the allocation stage, not through a FERA veto. That is a real and defensible point, and it is available to Classic without any of Track 2's apparatus.

### Finding 4 — Contemporaneous 1957 confirmation from a neutral third party.
**Serves: Track 2 (kills it) / Combined** — Grade **A**

**Source:** International Monetary Fund, *Eighth Annual Report on Exchange Restrictions* (1957), India country chapter, printed pp. 164–170, under "Imports and Import Payments." Already in project files as `primary_docs/IMF_8th_Annual_Report_1957_INDIA_CHAPTER_FULLTEXT.md`. Describes India's system as at end-1956.

**Verbatim:**
> "**Where a valid import license is held, the required exchange is released by authorized banks on presentation of the exchange control copy of the license.** The license holder may effect payments by opening letters of credit or by making remittances against sight drafts. Advance remittances in payment of imports before shipping documents are received are not normally permitted; but in special cases, e.g., imports of machinery and capital goods, where deposits have to be made with overseas manufacturers, the Reserve Bank grants special authorization for advance payment for a part of the value of the import."

**Confidence: STATED** for end-1956 / 1957 — i.e., **precisely the essay's pivot year**, and contemporaneous rather than retrospective.

**What it does to the claim:** closes the period gap between Finding 2 (1940–51) and Finding 1 (1990s). Three independent sources — the RBI's official history, the IMF's contemporaneous country survey, and the RBI's own governing manual — describe the same automatic, document-triggered, bank-executed mechanism across a fifty-year span. Track 2's mechanism claim has no surviving period in which it could have been true.

Note also the sentence's only reserved discretion: RBI special authorisation for **advance** payment before shipping documents arrive. That is a timing concession on an already-approved import, not a gate on the transaction. It is the exception that measures the rule.

### Finding 5 — Target 0.2: the capital-goods rescue also fails. Capital-goods RBI approvals attach to FINANCING, not to exchange release.
**Serves: Track 2 (kills the narrower version too)** — Grade **A**

**Source:** RBI *Exchange Control Manual*, Chapter 7, Parts A and B — full text of every RBI-approval requirement in the chapter, reviewed systematically.

The complete set of provisions in ECM Ch. 7 requiring Reserve Bank approval is:
- **7A.11** — deferred payment arrangements beyond six months from shipment ("All cases of extended payment terms require prior approval of Reserve Bank")
- **7A.12** — interest on import bills beyond six months
- **7A.22** — post-parcel imports where the goods are not of a kind normally sent by post, or where "authorised dealer is not satisfied about the bona fides of the application"
- **7A.27** — plant and machinery bought abroad "on 'as is where is basis'", where there are **no shipping documents** ("In the absence of shipping documents, it will not be possible for authorised dealers to open letters of credit or make remittances against such imports")
- **7A.28** — gold/platinum/silver by nominated agencies
- **7B.1** — foreign currency borrowing, under **Section 8(1) of FERA 1973**: "no person resident in India can borrow any foreign exchange from any person resident in or outside India without prior permission of Reserve Bank." The Manual's own note: "**The above procedure is also applicable for import of capital goods on financial lease basis.**"

**Confidence: STATED** (for the ECM's own period; same 1990s dating caveat as Finding 1).

**What it does to the claim:** kills Target 0.2's narrower rescue. Every capital-goods-specific RBI approval in the Manual is triggered by **how the import is financed** — deferred payment, suppliers'/buyers' credit, financial lease, foreign-currency borrowing — or by a **missing document** (no shipping documents on 'as is where is' plant). None of them is a gate on the exchange release for a straightforwardly-financed capital-goods import against a valid licence. A firm with an industrial licence and an import licence, paying cash against documents, hit no separate FERA decision.

The FERA 1973 s.8(1) borrowing provision is a genuine RBI discretionary power, and it is worth the essay noting — but it is a control on **external borrowing**, not on importing. Conflating the two would be exactly the overclaim this brief exists to prevent.

**Negative finding logged:** the 1985 Gazette item (`in.gazette.e.1985.99`) surfaced in search with promising language about "applications for import licences for substantial values of plant and machinery," which looked like it might evidence a value-threshold gate. Retrieved in full (1.3MB OCR) and checked — the Exchange Control Manual references in it do not establish a separate FERA exchange gate on capital goods. No support for Target 0.2 there either.

## TIER 1

### Finding 6 — ⭐ The Basic Travel Quota traces to the reserve position — Classic's own mechanism. This is the run's most valuable result.
**Serves: Classic / Combined** — Grade **A**

**Source:** *History of the Reserve Bank of India*, Vol. 1, Ch. 20, section "**Exchange for Foreign Travel, etc.**", pp. ~659–60.

**Verbatim:**
> "With the opening up of communications after the close of the war and the emphasis on the rapid industrialisation of the country, increased visits abroad by businessmen for purchase of plant and machinery... became necessary as well as practicable. To facilitate such visits, relaxations were made in the exchange control restrictions on foreign travel, including the abolition in February 1946 of the Exit (Finance) Permit system. **In September 1946, the Reserve Bank was authorised to deal with all applications for purposes of travel for business without reference to Government.** The rules for release of exchange for journeys abroad for medical treatment and for education were also liberalised in the immediate post-war period. **Foreign exchange was also granted for visits abroad for reasons of personal convenience, but the amounts varied according to the overall foreign exchange position and the degree of softness of the currencies of the countries visited.** In these as also in matters relating to release of exchange for emigrants, the policy of the Bank of England was followed closely."

**Confidence: STATED** — for the 1946–51 period. This is the RBI's official history describing the RBI's own administrative practice, in its own voice, with the mechanism stated as a general rule rather than a one-off.

**What it does to the claim — this is the answer to Target 1.5, and it is yes.** The brief asked whether the personal travel allowance's ceiling was set in direct response to the same reserve constraint Classic already documents, rather than being a generic feature of "the Licence Raj." The RBI's own history says the amounts "**varied according to the overall foreign exchange position**." That is the reserve position — the identical quantity that Classic's Lock A (RBI Act minimum reserve floor) and Lock B (IMF gold-subscription formula capping unconditional drawing rights) both operate on.

So the chain closes without borrowing anything from Track 2:
1. Sterling-area membership and its 1957 expiry determine India's usable reserve position (Classic, already proven).
2. The RBI Act reserve floor and the IMF drawing-rights cap constrain how much foreign exchange is available at all (Locks A and B, already proven).
3. **The personal-travel allowance is set as a direct function of that same "overall foreign exchange position" (this finding).**
4. Therefore the ordinary Indian's inability to leave the country with meaningful money is a downstream consequence of the exact mechanism Classic proves — not a vague attribute of the Raj.

**Two cautions the essay must observe, stated plainly:**

- **This is the BTQ's ancestor, not the BTQ under that name.** The passage describes exchange "for visits abroad for reasons of personal convenience" in 1946–51. It establishes the *principle* — allowance indexed to reserve position — as STATED for that period. Any claim that the specific instrument called the Basic Travel Quota, or any specific later figure, was set on the same basis is **INFERRED** unless separately sourced. Do not let the 1977 $108 figure inherit this finding's confidence tag without its own evidence.
- **The last sentence cuts against a nationalist reading and should not be suppressed:** "the policy of the Bank of England was followed closely." For travel and emigrant remittances specifically, India was copying UK practice. That supports "British in design" — a claim this project has already established repeatedly — and it does *not* support "British in 1957 causation."

### Finding 7 — The structural correction: FERA's real discretionary gate was on PERSONS, not on TRADE.
**Serves: Combined (replaces Track 2's overclaim with a defensible one)** — Grade **B**

**Source:** Synthesis of Findings 1–6, all from RBI official history Vol. 1 Ch. 20 and the ECM. Specific supporting passages:

On persons — RBI discretion is real and explicit:
> "Release of exchange for foreign travel and remittances for miscellaneous private purposes also came to be regulated from the middle of 1940. Subsequently, to check the evasion of the regulations through travel 'without any foreign exchange' resulting in deferred exchange liabilities to the hosts abroad, **a system of issue of Exit (Finance) Permits by the Reserve Bank (the forerunner of 'P' form) was introduced effective August 2, 1943.** The initial regulations in regard to remittances for other private purposes, such as maintenance of families abroad, transfer of capital and of interest, dividend and insurance premia were also **gradually made more stringent**, in line with the policy of the U.K. Control."

Against trade — no such discretion, per Findings 1–4.

**Confidence: CONVERGENT** — no single source states this asymmetry as a proposition; it is the consistent pattern across every source checked. Tagged as inference, not as a quoted finding.

**What it does to the claim:** this is what the essay should say **instead of** Track 2. FERA's discretionary machinery — the Exit (Finance) Permit, the 'P' form, the personal-convenience allowance indexed to the reserve position, emigrant transfer limits, maintenance remittances "gradually made more stringent" — bore on **individuals**. On trade, FERA's role was ministerial: banks released exchange against a document, and the actual rationing had already happened upstream at the allocation-and-licensing stage (Finding 3).

This is a **smaller** claim than "chief enabler of the Licence-Permit Raj." It is also true, evidenced, and — for an essay that wants human stakes — more useful, because it locates FERA's bite exactly where a reader feels it: on the person at the bank counter, not on the importing firm.

### Finding 8 — Target 2.1: Pakistan's omission of the sunset clause is confirmed from a second, Pakistan-side source — and the comparative is sharper than the essay currently has it.
**Serves: Classic** — Grade **B**

**Source:** *The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947 (VII of 1947)* — Pakistan consolidated text, as published at `http://nasirlawsite.com/laws/fexcregu.htm` (a Pakistani legal publisher, updated January 2014). Section 1.

**Verbatim** — Section 1, "Short title, extent and commencement," in full:
> "(1) This Act may be called the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947.
> (2) It extends to the whole of Pakistan and applies to all citizens of Pakistan and persons in the service of Government wherever they may be.
> (3) It shall come into force on such date as the Federal Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint in this behalf.
> **(4) [Omitted by the Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act, 1 of 1952, S. 2].**"

**Confidence: CONVERGENT — and deliberately not upgraded to STATED.** This is a second consolidated text attributing the omission of s.1(4) to a 1952 amending Act, which is better than the single Bangladesh footnote the claim previously rested on. But **the two sources are not fully independent**: Bangladesh inherited Pakistan's statute book, so both consolidations descend from the same underlying Pakistani amendment. This is corroboration of transcription, not of substance. **The amending Act itself (Pakistan Act 1 of 1952) still has not been read.** Do not cite this as STATED.

**What it does to the claim:** confirms the Pakistan comparative stands, and supplies the amending Act's exact citation — **Act 1 of 1952, section 2** — which is what a future pass needs to request from the Pakistan Code or a library.

**And it sharpens the comparative considerably.** Earlier in this project I recovered India's own 1952 amendment: the **Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act, VIII of 1952**, moved by C. D. Deshmukh on 14 February 1952, which *extended* India's sunset to 31 December 1957 (see `primary_docs/ProvisionalParliament_1952-02-14_Deshmukh_FERA_Amendment_Bill_FULLTEXT.md`). So:

| | India | Pakistan |
|---|---|---|
| Amending Act | Act **VIII** of 1952 | Act **1** of 1952 |
| What it did to the sunset clause | **Extended** it to 31 Dec 1957 | **Omitted** it entirely |

**Both successor states amended the same inherited statute in the same year, and chose oppositely.** That is a materially stronger comparative than "India 1957, Pakistan 1952," because it removes the five-year gap that an unsympathetic reader could attribute to drift, timing, or administrative happenstance. It is the same statute, the same year, the same clause — and a fork. This directly answers the "generic administrative continuity" attack the brief identifies as the comparative's job.

**Caveat to preserve:** India's 1952 extension was itself driven by a stated *ultra vires* worry about the original delegated extension power (Deshmukh's own words), not by a considered choice to keep the sunset alive on the merits. The fork is real, but India's side of it was partly a lawyer's correction. Say so.

## Incidental captures

**I1 — The Exchange Control Manual's own origin date, and FERA's relationship to it.** RBI Vol. 1, p. ~354: "The initial instructions issued by the Bank in respect of exchange control as well as those issued from time to time modifying, deleting or adding to them were codified and published in the form of an **Exchange Control Manual in June 1940**; revised editions of the Manual were issued from time to time." *Significance:* the exchange-control machinery was codified **seven years before FERA 1947**, under Defence of India Rules. This independently corroborates the Kamtekar position the brief says is already established (wartime origin) — and it means FERA 1947 codified an existing administrative apparatus rather than creating one. Useful for Classic's framing of what the 1957 permanence actually made permanent.

**I2 — India's control was deliberately looser than Britain's, by choice, on the Governor's reasoning.** RBI Vol. 1, p. ~354ff, contrasting the two systems: in the UK "licences to deal in foreign exchange were limited to a few leading British joint-stock banks... The authorised dealers operated as direct agents of the Bank of England." The Indian Governor's stated object was to "make India as a whole a source of strength to the sterling area rather than weakness, **without endeavouring to tighten control to such an extent that it might defeat its own object**." *Significance:* a documented instance of India declining to match UK control stringency — relevant to any claim about British dictation of Indian control design.

**I3 — The 1947 licensing collapse, in the RBI's own words.** RBI Vol. 1, p. ~647: after the March 1947 cancellation of O.G.L. VIII, "**there was almost complete chaos**"; the confusion "continued till practically the end of 1947," and the Finance Department's response — that "in the period of administrative change through which we have been passing, a certain amount of confusion was inevitable" — "was not relished by the Bank." *Significance:* vivid, quotable, and dated to the FERA enactment year.

**I4 — A clean, non-OCR sterling figure and the Rs/£ conversion basis.** RBI's *The Problems of Plenty, 1947-56* (in project files) opens: "India's wartime sterling balances amounted to over **£1,134 million (Rs 1,512 crores)** on the eve of independence," with the footnote: "**Until 6 June 1966, a rupee was worth 18 pence, and a pound equalled Rs 13.33.**" *Significance:* this is the conversion basis the brief demands be cited for any currency figure. Any £/Rs conversion in the essay for the 1947–66 period should cite **£1 = Rs 13.33** and attribute it to this footnote. Note this figure (£1,134m at independence) also sanity-checks the OCR-damaged "£169 millions" in the December 1950 Deshmukh statement (H8), which was already flagged as a probable dropped leading digit.

**I5 — Deshmukh's own IMF drawing-rights figure, 1947.** RBI Vol. 1, p. ~646, in the Governor's reasoning on why import control needed reform: India could not afford a large adverse balance of payments "especially as **drawings from the I.M.F., permissible up to Rs. 33 crores in a full year**, would have to be reserved for meeting unexpected deficits caused by monsoon failures or other unforeseen contingencies." *Significance:* directly relevant to Classic's Lock B (IMF drawing-rights cap). This is a contemporaneous 1947 statement of the annual drawing ceiling, from the Governor, treating it as a reserve of last resort against monsoon failure — which is also a striking anticipation of exactly what happened in 1965–67.
